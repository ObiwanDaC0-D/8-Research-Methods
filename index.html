SAMPLING LAB

Sampling variability and selection bias

Generate a synthetic population with a known mean. Compare a simple random sample against a systematically selected sample that overrepresents high values.

The selection-biased sample is deliberately unrealistic to make the distinction between random error and systematic bias visible. Increasing sample size alone does not eliminate selection bias.

STATISTICAL INFERENCE & EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN · V3

From sample evidence to defensible conclusions

Explore uncertainty, hypothesis testing, statistical power, standardized effect sizes, and randomized experiments through synthetic simulations. These examples use normal-theory assumptions and are not a substitute for analysis of real study data.

01 · CONFIDENCE INTERVAL COVERAGE

How often do intervals capture the true mean?

Draw repeated independent samples from a normal population with mean 50 and standard deviation 12. Intervals use the known population standard deviation and normal critical values.

A 95% confidence procedure covers the fixed parameter in approximately 95% of repeated samples; it does not assign a 95% probability to the parameter for a realized interval.

02 · HYPOTHESIS TESTING

Test a population mean

Illustrative two-sided z-test with a known population standard deviation of 12. The p-value assumes the null hypothesis and the test model.

03 · STATISTICAL POWER & EFFECT SIZE

Explore the design tradeoffs

Approximate two-sided z-test power for a difference between two independent group means with equal group sizes, common known standard deviation, and a normally distributed test statistic.

Effect-size conventions are contextual, not universal. Power is a pre-study property of a specified test and assumed true effect; it is not the probability that a significant finding is true.

04 · RANDOMIZED EXPERIMENT

Assignment, treatment effects, and random variation

Simulate a balanced randomized trial. Each participant has a baseline outcome generated from a normal distribution, with a fixed treatment effect added only in the treatment group. Results vary across runs.

03 · RELIABILITY & ITEM ANALYSIS

Internal consistency laboratory

Use synthetic Likert responses or import numeric item responses as CSV. Each row is a respondent; each selected numeric column is an item. This lab calculates Cronbach's alpha, corrected item–total correlations, and alpha-if-deleted. It does not establish unidimensionality or construct validity.

04 · CONSTRUCT VALIDITY

Build a validity argument

Validity concerns interpretations and uses of scores—not a permanent property of a questionnaire. Document evidence and potential threats rather than relying on a single statistic.

05 · SURVEY SAMPLING & NONRESPONSE

Precision versus representation

Illustrative simple random sampling margin of error for a proportion near 50%, assuming a large population and 95% normal-approximation confidence. A design effect scales variance; neither this calculation nor a large sample fixes nonresponse or coverage bias.

02 · CONFOUNDING ADJUSTMENT

Compare crude and adjusted estimates

Estimate the X₁ association with the outcome alone, then compare it with a model including X₂. This demonstrates coefficient changes under adjustment—not whether X₂ is a genuine confounder. Use causal theory and a DAG to determine an appropriate adjustment set.

03 · INTERACTION EFFECTS

Explore effect modification

When an interaction is enabled, the association of X₁ with the outcome varies with X₂. For logistic models, this is a change in log-odds, not a constant probability difference.
